Dancers' Alley

What Lies Across & Sydney Cypher Map

Two connected interactive installations for Sydney's Dancers' Alley — one indoor, one outdoor — that turn a commuter shortcut into a space where street dancers and university students can be seen, feel safe, and belong.

My Role Ideation · Storyboard illustration (both concepts) · User-testing data logging & analysis · Ethics documentation
Team 4 designers — IDEA9202 Tutorial 3, Group 1
Deliverable 2 hi-fi interactive prototypes · 2 rounds of user testing · 14 participants

Overview

Why Dancers' Alley

Dancers' Alley, beneath the ICC Sydney bridge in Darling Harbour, is already an informal rehearsal ground for the city's street dance community and a daily shortcut for university students from nearby campuses — two groups research shows are disproportionately at risk of anxiety, body-image pressure and social isolation. Rather than design one generic wellbeing app, we split the response across the site's two natural zones: an enclosed bridge underpass and an open plaza. What Lies Across uses the underpass for a private, mirror-based moment of self-expression; Sydney Cypher Map uses the plaza to turn a solo freestyle into a public, celebrated mark on a shared digital map of the city.

How might we support emerging street dancers to strengthen their self-image and sense of belonging in shared urban spaces?

Research & Persona

From a broad mental-health brief to a specific one

A CRAAP test and MoSCoW matrix on candidate suburbs, then a proto-persona that proved too broad, led us to narrow the brief to street dancers specifically.

Angela Huang, 24

UTS student · Second-generation East Asian · Pre-professional street dancer

"I'm learning to be seen without feeling like I have to be perfect. I want to find my people and feel safe enough to be myself while I get better."

Goals

  • Build belonging and an authentic identity as a street dancer — "not just a studio kid"
  • Grow self-esteem through small public wins
  • Practise safely in public without unwanted filming

Pain Points

  • Compares herself to advanced dancers; hesitates to join group performance
  • Wants to be seen but dreads unsolicited filming or comments
  • Family favours academic success over dance as identity

Motivations

  • A crew-like circle that values growth over perfection
  • Uses dance as a mental reset, not a performance to be judged
  • Contribute gently — playlist curation, small jams — without needing the spotlight

Opportunities

  • Softer lighting zones, groove-first prompts, "first-timer friendly" corners
  • Playful "ask before you film" signage and an audience code of respect
  • Community activities that encourage cyphers, freestyle, battles

Ethical Considerations

Rhetorical design for indoor and outdoor

Ethos

Continuous power use and hardware waste from screens and sensors; privacy risk if user images are stored or broadcast without consent; installing on the bridge can disrupt its normal pedestrian use. We reframed street dance as a community art form worth that trade-off, not urban disturbance.

Pathos

Public performance can heighten social pressure and imposter syndrome if the prompts feel judgmental, and repeated digital validation risks turning growth into gamification. Done well, it strengthens peer bonds and builds empathy between diverse, previously separate user groups.

Logos

Technical breakdowns and unclear framing of "daily challenges" can confuse or frustrate users; residents, authorities and dancers of different skill levels have competing claims on the same public space. The response needed clear, logical design for safe, inclusive interaction.

What Lies Across

Indoor · Reflection through movement

A closed, mirror-lined stretch of the underpass, reframed as a place to feel first and articulate meaning only if you choose to.

The concept reframes reflection from self-conscious judgment to embodied presence. Instead of asking dancers to analyse themselves, the space lets them first feel, and only later choose whether to articulate meaning. By combining real mirrors with a digital counterpart, the installation acknowledges both the technical discipline of the dancer and the emotional, imaginative self that exists beneath form. "What Lies Across" is not a question to be answered intellectually, but through movement.

01

Low-fi prototype

Cardboard and A4 paper, titled "Who Am I?", tested to probe the overall concept and provoke self-reflection.

02

Tutor feedback

Too vague, lacked playfulness; the persona was too broad and needed sharper research.

03

Hi-fi v1 · 14 participants

Physical + digital mirrors built in p5.js, post-it prompts and pencils, tested in a closed "dance corridor" room.

04

Refinement

Real-time (not delayed) motion tracking, personal music choice, and a QR-code note wall to protect privacy.

Key insight: despite enjoying the testing phase, dancers don't seek reflection — they dance to forget. The act of dancing is itself their form of reflection. So the refined installation stopped prompting analytical self-reflection, and instead let reflection be felt through movement.

Sydney Cypher Map

Outdoor · Belonging through collective presence

An open plaza installation that turns a one-minute freestyle into a permanent, glowing mark on a live map of the community.

The Sydney Cypher Map transforms individual performances into a shared digital artwork, turning individual expression into visible community presence. By inviting people to move, play, and learn about street dance culture, it makes the space more inclusive and alive — easing imposter syndrome, social stigma, and fear of judgement by showing that every contribution matters.

01

Hi-fi v1

Five connected elements: QR poster ("Yo! Dancer"), motion-tracked screen, evolving live map, mobile registration, and a marked ground zone.

02

Round 1 · 7 participants

Non-dancers didn't identify with "dancer" on the QR poster; "cypher" read as unfamiliar jargon to newcomers.

03

Iteration to v2

Reworded to the inclusive "Let's dance!", aligned the map visual to the screen, added a circular boundary and a 30-second timer.

04

Round 2 · 3 participants

Cohesive visual identity landed well; users wanted adjustable timing and music that matched their own style.

Storyboard

Passive
Passive

When no one is dancing, City Cypher Map rests in passive mode — displaying a live map, pulsating visuals that hint at the hidden rhythms of the street dance community, together with QR-code posters inviting passers-by to step forward and awaken it.

The Sydney Cypher Map operates in three modes to suit different scenarios: a passive mode displaying ambient visuals when no one is dancing, an individual mode that responds to an individual's movement with light and sound feedback, and a multiple participant mode that amplifies group energy and interaction.
Individual
Step 1

When a dancer steps into Dancers' Alley, they begin by scanning a QR code placed along the path and on the wall beneath the digital screen.

Step 2

The link opens a simple website that explains the challenge and guides them through a few quick steps to personalise their session.

Step 3

Once ready, the dancer steps into the marked circle, inspired by the open cypher tradition where the community forms a ring of support.

Step 4

As they move, the system tracks their motion and transforms it into a digital echo of their energy projected live on the screen. On their phone, they can see the timer and opt out at any time.

Step 5

When the dance ends, the Sydney Cypher Map reappears, highlighting the participant's location and adding a new glowing mark.

Multiple
Step 1

When multiple people participate, one participant scans and registers on behalf of the group.

Step 2

One participant customises the session with their chosen music, style and duration.

Step 3

As the cypher begins, the system recognises each dancer's motion and assigns them a unique colour. The visuals intertwine and pulse together, forming a living artwork of connection.

Step 4

Similar to individual mode, when the dance ends, the Sydney Cypher Map reappears, highlighting all participants' locations by adding new glowing marks.

Step 5

Same as individual mode, if participants choose to opt in their email, they will receive a recording of their performance to keep or share later.

Final design board

Design solution

Sydney Cypher Map · Outdoor · Final design

Interface moments

The experience is carried through a small set of interface moments. Each screen is paired with a short set of footnotes so the visuals and the narrative stay connected.

Onboarding screen

An inclusive entry point that explains the activity and invites first-time participants in.

  • “Let’s dance!” keeps the tone playful and low-pressure.
  • Optional postcode and email fields make privacy and consent visible.
  • Simple steps reduce hesitation and support confident participation.

Ready state

A calm transition screen that prepares the participant before the experience begins.

  • Countdown and visible timer create a sense of readiness and control.
  • Music, duration and style choices allow personalisation without pressure.
  • The interface keeps the focus on movement rather than performance.

Affirmation state

The experience finishes by turning the session into a visible, shared moment of belonging.

  • Participant count reinforces community impact and collective presence.
  • Visual affirmation helps users feel seen without needing to perform perfectly.
  • Feedback and share options make the experience feel reflective rather than purely transactional.
